\begin{abstract}
This report derives the unit dual quaternion representation of a rigid 6-DOF
pose, implements it as a reusable Unity C\# component, and uses it to compare
dual-quaternion pose blending against decoupled position/rotation
interpolation. The dual-quaternion blend is shown, both analytically and
through a numerical experiment, to follow a coupled screw-motion path rather
than the straight chord produced by interpolating position and rotation
independently.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Rigid-body poses in VR --- controller transforms, tracked limbs, networked
avatar updates --- are usually represented as a rotation and a translation
kept in separate variables. Interpolating the two independently is simple but
physically inconsistent: it does not correspond to any single rigid motion of
the object between the two poses. A \emph{unit dual quaternion} instead
encodes rotation and translation as one coupled algebraic object, so that
interpolating it produces a genuine rigid motion at every step. This report
covers three things: the derivation of the dual quaternion pose
representation and its inverse (Section~\ref{sec:math}), a Unity C\#
implementation of it (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}), and a small
experiment comparing it against decoupled interpolation
(Section~\ref{sec:experiment}).

\section{Mathematical Formulation}
\label{sec:math}

\subsection{Constructing the unit dual quaternion}

Let $q_r$ be a unit rotation quaternion ($\lVert q_r \rVert = 1$) and let
$t = (t_x, t_y, t_z)$ be a translation vector, embedded as the pure quaternion
$T = (t_x, t_y, t_z, 0)$. A dual quaternion is written $\hat q = q_r + \epsilon
q_d$ with $\epsilon^2 = 0$.

A pure rotation has zero dual part, $\hat R = q_r + \epsilon \cdot 0$, and a
pure translation is written $\hat T = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon T$. Applying the
rotation first and the translation second gives the composite pose
$\hat q = \hat T \hat R$:
\[
\hat{q} = \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon T\right)(q_r + 0\cdot\epsilon)
        = q_r + \epsilon\left(\tfrac{1}{2} T q_r\right).
\]

This gives the pose representation used throughout the rest of the report:
\begin{equation}
\hat{q} = q_r + \epsilon q_d, \qquad q_d = \tfrac{1}{2} T q_r.
\label{eq:pose}
\end{equation}
Since $\lVert q_r \rVert = 1$ and $q_r \cdot q_d = \operatorname{Re}(q_d q_r^{*})
= \operatorname{Re}(\tfrac12 T) = 0$, $\hat q$ satisfies both constraints of a
unit dual quaternion and validly encodes the 6-DOF pose $(R, t)$.

\subsection{Recovering rotation and translation}

The real part directly gives the rotation, renormalized to remove
floating-point drift:
\[
R = \frac{q_r}{\lVert q_r \rVert}.
\]
To recover the translation, start from $q_d = \tfrac12 T q_r$ in
Equation~\eqref{eq:pose} and right-multiply by $q_r^{-1}$. Since $q_r$ is
unit, $q_r^{-1} = q_r^{*}$, so
\[
2 q_d q_r^{*} = T (q_r q_r^{*}) = T
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
T = 2 q_d q_r^{*}.
\]
The vector part of the resulting pure quaternion $T$ is the recovered
translation $t = (T_x, T_y, T_z)$; its scalar component is zero up to
floating-point error.

\subsection{Composition}

Two poses compose as
\[
\hat{q}_a \hat{q}_b = (q_{r,a} q_{r,b}) + \epsilon (q_{r,a} q_{d,b} + q_{d,a} q_{r,b}),
\]
which applies $b$ first, then $a$: $R = R_a R_b$ and $t = R_a t_b + t_a$,
matching Unity's own quaternion/transform composition order.

\subsection{Why dual quaternions for VR blending}

Natural VR motions --- a controller swinging through an arc, a shoulder,
elbow and wrist chain articulating together --- combine rotation and
translation along a single curved path rather than two independent
straight-line channels. Blending position and rotation separately
(\texttt{Vector3.Lerp} together with \texttt{Quaternion.Slerp}) forces the
object along a flat chord between the two endpoints while the orientation
turns independently of it, which is the same decoupling responsible for
``candy-wrapper'' mesh collapse in skinning and for visibly unnatural sliding
motion on a single rigid object. A dual quaternion blends translation and
rotation as one coupled quantity, so the interpolated pose instead follows
the true spatial screw motion between the two poses (Chasles' theorem).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The representation above was implemented in Unity C\# as a
\texttt{DualQuaternion} struct with two \texttt{Quaternion} fields, \texttt{r}
(real part) and \texttt{d} (dual part), and the following methods:

\begin{itemize}
\raggedright
  \item \texttt{FromPose(rotation, translation)} builds
    $\hat q = q_r + \epsilon(\tfrac12 T q_r)$, Equation~\eqref{eq:pose}.
  \item \texttt{ToPose(out rotation, out translation)} extracts
    $R = q_r/\lVert q_r\rVert$ and $t = 2 q_d q_r^{*}$.
  \item \texttt{Multiply(a, b)} performs the rigid composition derived above.
  \item \texttt{Slerp(a, b, u)} blends two poses: shortest-path sign
    alignment on the real part, \texttt{Quaternion.Slerp} on $q_r$, a
    componentwise \texttt{Mathf.Lerp} on $q_d$, then re-normalization. This is
    dual quaternion linear blending (DLB), an approximation to true
    constant-speed screw interpolation (ScLERP) that is standard practice for
    this kind of assignment.
  \item \texttt{Normalized()} and \texttt{Conjugate()} enforce
    $\lVert q_r\rVert = 1$ and $q_r \cdot q_d = 0$, and provide the dual
    quaternion conjugate used internally.
\end{itemize}

Two details were easy to get wrong and are worth recording. First, Unity's
\texttt{Quaternion} type has no operator for addition or for scalar
multiplication between two quaternions, so every dual-part operation had to
be written out componentwise rather than through \texttt{Quaternion}
arithmetic. Second, the dual part of the identity pose is the \emph{zero}
quaternion $(0,0,0,0)$, not \texttt{Quaternion.identity} (which is
$(0,0,0,1)$); using the latter would silently encode a phantom translation.
For the same reason, \texttt{Slerp} blends the dual part with
\texttt{Mathf.Lerp} component-by-component rather than
\texttt{Quaternion.Lerp}, which renormalizes its result to a unit quaternion
and would corrupt the generally non-unit dual part.

\subsection{Demo scene}

An editor script adds the menu command \textbf{AM5011 $\to$ Build Full Demo
Scene}, which constructs the scene programmatically: a fixed \texttt{PoseA}
and \texttt{PoseB} anchor, an \texttt{InterpolatedPose} object driven by
\texttt{DualQuaternion.Slerp}, a \texttt{DecoupledPose} object driven by plain
\texttt{Vector3.Lerp} and \texttt{Quaternion.Slerp} for comparison, a UI
slider bound to the interpolation parameter $u$, and a \texttt{TrailRenderer}
on each moving object to visualize the swept path.

A separate Play-mode test component checks that \texttt{FromPose}/
\texttt{ToPose} round-trips correctly, that \texttt{Slerp} exactly recovers
both endpoints at $u=0,1$, that the blended pose remains a valid unit dual
quaternion at $u=0.5$, and that \texttt{Multiply} composes two poses the same
way Unity's own transform chaining does.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Setup}

To isolate the effect of coupling, \texttt{PoseA} was placed at
$(-2, 0, 0)$~m with identity rotation and \texttt{PoseB} at $(2, 0, 0)$~m
rotated $120^\circ$ about $+Z$. Both interpolation methods were evaluated on
this identical pair of endpoints: (1) \emph{separate} interpolation
(\texttt{Vector3.Lerp} on position, \texttt{Quaternion.Slerp} on rotation),
and (2) \emph{dual-quaternion} interpolation
(\texttt{DualQuaternion.Slerp(dqA, dqB, u)}). The underlying math was
re-implemented independently in Python to sample both paths at 101 values of
$u \in [0,1]$ and generate Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} and
Table~\ref{tab:results}.

\subsection{Observations}

\begin{itemize}
\raggedright
  \item \textbf{Curved vs.\ straight path.} The separate method's position
    follows the straight chord $y=0$ between the anchors, while the
    dual-quaternion blend follows a curved arc dipping to $y=-1.0$~m at
    $u=0.5$. Coupling rotation into the translation term
    ($q_d = \tfrac12 T q_r$) is what bends the path.
  \item \textbf{Orientation timing is unaffected here.} Both methods compute
    rotation as \texttt{Quaternion.Slerp} of the same endpoint quaternions,
    so the angle-versus-$u$ curve matches exactly in this experiment; the two
    methods diverge only in where the translation goes, not in rotation
    timing. For a genuine screw motion, this position/rotation coupling is
    exactly what decoupled interpolation cannot express.
  \item \textbf{Naturalness for VR.} The curved path is consistent with a
    joint (shoulder, elbow, wrist) sweeping a controller through an arc; the
    straight-chord path looks mechanical and unnatural for a rotating hand or
    tool, since it is only correct when translation and rotation happen to be
    physically independent.
  \item \textbf{Rigidity is preserved either way.} Because
    \texttt{Normalized()} is applied after every blend, the dual-quaternion
    interpolation stays on the unit dual-quaternion manifold throughout
    $u \in [0,1]$, with no collapse or scale drift at the midpoint.
  \item \textbf{Caveat.} This simple DLB (linear blend followed by
    re-normalization) approximates true constant-speed ScLERP; for larger
    rotation angles or more widely separated anchors the coupling becomes
    even more pronounced, which is the same effect exploited to reduce
    skinning artifacts in avatar deformation \cite{kavan2008}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Representing a 6-DOF pose as a unit dual quaternion couples rotation and
translation into a single interpolable object. The derivation in
Section~\ref{sec:math} shows this representation is exact and invertible; the
implementation in Section~\ref{sec:implementation} shows it fits cleanly into
Unity's existing \texttt{Quaternion}/\texttt{Vector3} API once the
componentwise dual-part arithmetic is handled correctly; and the experiment
in Section~\ref{sec:experiment} shows the practical consequence of that
coupling: a curved, physically coherent motion path in place of the straight
chord produced by interpolating position and rotation separately. This is the
property that makes dual quaternions useful for VR controller smoothing,
avatar skinning, and networked pose updates.
